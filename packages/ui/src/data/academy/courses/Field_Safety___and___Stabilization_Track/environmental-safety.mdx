Climate hazards are operational hazards. This course prepares pods to operate safely through heat, cold, smoke, floods, and storms — using simple, low-cost strategies to keep people alive and effective.

## Overview
Environmental danger is one of the most overlooked threats in mutual aid and direct action.  
Dehydration, heat stroke, smoke inhalation, and hypothermia can disable a team faster than confrontation.

This course trains pods to:
- Read local weather risks in advance.
- Integrate environmental monitoring into logistics.
- Equip volunteers for extreme conditions.
- Build resilience into every deployment plan.

> “Preparedness isn’t paranoia — it’s care in motion.”

## Learn & Do

### 1. Understanding Environmental Risks
Common field hazards by type:

| Threat | Common Context | Primary Risks |
|--------|----------------|---------------|
| ☀️ Heat & Sun | Long rallies, aid stations, disaster cleanup | Dehydration, heat stroke, sunburn |
| 🌧 Flooding | Heavy rain, storm surges | Contamination, drowning, hypothermia |
| ❄️ Cold & Snow | Overnight vigils, rural support | Frostbite, shock, low dexterity |
| 🔥 Smoke & Fire | Wildfire zones, police gas deployment | Lung irritation, asthma, disorientation |
| 🌪 Wind & Debris | Storms, tear gas crowd dispersal | Flying hazards, eye injuries |

<Callout type="hint">
Each region should maintain a **Seasonal Safety Profile** — a 1-page summary of local climate hazards and thresholds for cancelling or scaling operations.
</Callout>

### 2. Heat Safety Protocols
Symptoms of heat illness often mimic anxiety or exhaustion.

| Stage | Signs | Action |
|-------|--------|--------|
| Heat Cramps | Muscle spasms, fatigue | Hydrate with electrolytes, rest in shade |
| Heat Exhaustion | Dizziness, headache, nausea | Move to shade, cool skin with damp cloths |
| Heat Stroke | Confusion, rapid pulse, no sweat | Emergency — cool immediately and evacuate |

**Key prevention steps:**
- 1 liter of water per hour of moderate activity.  
- Add electrolyte packets for every 2–3 liters.  
- Rest 10 minutes per hour of sun exposure.  
- Wear light-colored, breathable layers and hats.

> Rule of thumb: If you’re thirsty, you’re already dehydrated.

---

### 3. Cold Safety Protocols
Cold exposure kills silently. Prevention is coordination.

- Dress in **3 layers**: base (dry), insulation, outer shell.  
- Avoid cotton — it holds moisture.  
- Watch for **the “umbles”**: stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles — early hypothermia.  
- Swap damp socks and gloves immediately.  
- Create **warming zones** with blankets and chemical packs.  
- Always have **dry spares** for key volunteers.

<Callout type="warning">
If someone stops shivering, becomes confused, or clumsy, treat as hypothermia.  
Warm slowly — never rub frozen skin.
</Callout>

### 4. Air Quality & Smoke Readiness
Airborne particles can affect everyone differently.

- Track **AQI (Air Quality Index)** with local apps or AirNow.gov.  
- At AQI >100: Masks required.  
- At AQI >150: Move to indoor or vehicle operation.  
- Stock **N95 masks** and **saline spray** in every kit.  
- Prepare “Clean Air Corners” using fans with **MERV-13 filters**.  
- Seal tent or bus gaps with painter’s tape during wildfire events.

> Air hazards are invisible but cumulative — limit exposure time.

---

### 5. Flood & Storm Readiness
**Before deployment:**
- Check 24-hour rainfall forecasts and street flood maps.  
- Identify **high ground** evacuation zones.  
- Store electronics and first aid in watertight bins or drybags.  
- Use **rubber boots**, not cloth sneakers.  
- Never walk through water you can’t see the bottom of — 6 inches can knock a person over.

**During deployment:**
- Watch for **power lines, oil sheens, and sewage runoff**.  
- Mark safe rally points on higher elevation.  
- Keep vehicle keys, cash, and radios sealed and on-body.

---

### 6. Shelter and Shade Engineering
Low-cost materials save lives:
- Tarps + paracord = instant shelter.  
- Reflective emergency blankets double as radiant barriers.  
- Pallets keep supplies off wet ground.  
- Ventilate any covered space — heat rises fast.

<Callout type="hint">
Assign a **Shade & Shelter Lead** in your pod during all outdoor deployments. Their job: rotate shaded rest, hydration, and cooling zones every 2 hours.
</Callout>

---

### 7. Monitoring Volunteer Health
Create a **wellness check cycle**:
- Every 30 minutes in heat or smoke.  
- Every hour in cold or wet conditions.  
- Ask: “How’s your head, your water, your body?”  
- Track symptoms of dizziness, fatigue, nausea, chills, or disorientation.

Use a quick **color code** during field checks:
- 🟢 Good  
- 🟡 Slowing down  
- 🔴 Needs relief  

If two or more team members hit 🔴, **pause the operation**.

---

### 8. Emergency Environmental Response
If the environment suddenly shifts (storm, gas, wildfire):
1. Gather team at pre-marked rally point.  
2. Account for everyone — use headcount, not devices.  
3. Decide: shelter-in-place or retreat.  
4. Communicate status to dispatch.  
5. Log location and exposure times for after-action review.

---

### 9. Equipment Checklist
**Minimum for all deployments:**
- Reusable water jugs + electrolyte mix  
- N95 masks or respirators  
- Mylar blankets + tarps  
- Extra socks, gloves, hats  
- Sunscreen, bug spray, and hand sanitizer  
- Drybags, duct tape, and permanent markers  
- Portable thermometer and humidity meter  

---

### 10. Environmental Debrief & Data Hygiene
After deployment:
- Record temperature, weather, and hazards encountered.  
- Note how many volunteers reported exposure symptoms.  
- Evaluate gear — what failed or succeeded?  
- Rotate expired items and replace used supplies.  
- Share findings with neighboring pods to improve readiness.

> Environmental intelligence is shared safety.
