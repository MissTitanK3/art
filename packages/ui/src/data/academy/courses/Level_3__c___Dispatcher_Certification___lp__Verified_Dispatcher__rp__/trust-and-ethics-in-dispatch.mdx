# 🧠 Trust and Ethics in Dispatch
<TrackBadge variant="pod-leadership" />
<TrackBadge variant="movement-strategy" />

<Callout type="info">
In dispatch work, your choices shape real-world safety.  
Ethics and trust aren’t soft skills — they are operational foundations.  
This course trains you to lead with integrity, humility, and clarity.
</Callout>

---

## Overview

Every activation depends on trust.  
- Trust that the report is real.  
- Trust that responders are ready.  
- Trust that dispatchers protect information and people with care.

If that chain weakens, harm spreads fast.  
This module builds ethical reflexes for field coordination under pressure.

---

## Learn & Do

### Why Trust Matters

Trust sustains the entire ART system.  
Each role has a duty:

- **Reporters** verify information  
- **Responders** act safely and calmly  
- **Dispatchers** evaluate, communicate, and decide with care  

<Callout type="warning">
Your authority exists to protect — not to impress, punish, or control.
</Callout>

---

### Activating the Right People

Only activate responders who:
- Are trained for the needed role  
- Are geographically and emotionally ready  
- Have proven reliability or trusted vouch  
- Are not in direct conflict with the site or community  

If no safe option exists:
- Delay until confirmed observers arrive  
- Alert only a **small, high-trust subgroup**  
- Escalate to regional or admin support  

> ⚖️ Speed without safety destroys trust. Slow clarity saves it.

---

### Handling Sensitive Information

Dispatchers access high-risk data — names, addresses, trauma details, immigration info.  
Handle it as if lives depend on it.

**Do:**
- Log only in encrypted systems  
- Use initials or anonymized notes  
- Confirm consent before sharing names or photos  

**Don’t:**
- Discuss ops outside secure channels  
- Share screenshots  
- Assume consent from silence  

<Callout type="hint">
Documentation hygiene: Use minimal, time-bound retention.  
Delete raw data after review. Never archive chats in shared apps.
</Callout>

---

### Cultural Humility & Power Awareness

You may lead people with different race, language, or status backgrounds.  
Ethical leadership requires humility and listening:

- Ask pronouns, respect lived context  
- Avoid pressure-based “urgency” tones  
- Center frontline voices in decisions  
- Reflect on bias before assigning roles  

<Callout type="info">
Being “in charge” means accountable, not always correct.
</Callout>

---

### Balancing Urgency & Harm Reduction

When the clock is ticking:
1. Slow the pace, breathe.  
2. Choose smaller, safer activations.  
3. Communicate that caution is intentional.  
4. Document what was delayed and why.  

> “We’re pausing activation until verified.” is ethical dispatching — not hesitation.

---

### Protecting the Movement

Every message represents the movement’s credibility.  
Misuse of access or bad calls fracture collective safety.

- Never share unverified alerts  
- Use the minimum viable audience  
- Admit uncertainty out loud  
- Ask for peer review when unsure  

> You hold a gate, not a stage.

---

### Field Scripts

- “We’re holding activation until confirmation.”  
- “I need a de-escalation-trained responder only.”  
- “Don’t post this publicly — we’re coordinating privately.”  
- “I’m unsure — escalating to zone lead.”  

---

### Quick Ethics Checklist

- [ ] Verified info source  
- [ ] Appropriate responder activated  
- [ ] Consent verified for any data shared  
- [ ] Language clear, non-coercive  
- [ ] Decision documented transparently
