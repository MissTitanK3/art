# 🏕 Choosing Safe & Strategic CDC Locations
<TrackBadge variant="logistics" />
<TrackBadge variant="community-care" />

<Callout type="info">
The right location can make or break your Community Defense Center (CDC). This course helps pods evaluate visibility, safety, and logistics to build stable, trusted bases of operation.
</Callout>

---

## 🌍 Overview

Community Defense Centers (CDCs) are **public-facing hubs for care, safety, and coordination**.  
Choosing the right site balances **visibility, accessibility, and safety** — ensuring operations can serve people *without* creating new risks.

This guide is built from successful CDC setups in **shopping centers, parks, and apartment complexes**, and can be adapted to nearly any local environment.

---

## 🧭 Learn & Do

- Identify which **type of space fits your operation’s goals**
- Assess **site access, visibility, and risk**
- Navigate **permission, trust, and surveillance concerns**
- Follow the **complete setup-to-teardown process checklist**

---

## 🏗 Site Type Deep Dive

### 🏬 1. Shopping Centers
**Best for:** Visibility, short-term pop-ups, high foot traffic

**Strengths**
- Large parking lots and easy vehicle access  
- Restrooms and power may be nearby  
- Casual public presence — less likely to alarm  

**Risks**
- Property management may ask to leave suddenly  
- Cameras and police patrols common  
- Noise and crowds can limit privacy  

**Tips**
- Choose lots with open visibility but multiple exit routes  
- Set up near neutral businesses (grocers, laundromats, bus stops)  
- Bring portable shade, folding tables, and banners — *not tents* if management is strict  

---

### 🌳 2. Public Parks
**Best for:** Visibility with relaxed atmosphere; accessible to families and unhoused people

**Strengths**
- Free and accessible  
- Community already familiar with space  
- Good for repeated presence and casual interactions  

**Risks**
- Limited power, water, or shelter  
- Public restrooms may close early or be locked  
- Vulnerable to weather and crowd disruptions  

**Tips**
- Choose spots near shaded areas, water fountains, or community centers  
- Avoid corners near parking lots (isolation risk)  
- Scout weekend crowd behavior before deployment  

---

### 🏘 3. Apartment Complexes
**Best for:** Direct neighborhood outreach and trust building

**Strengths**
- Meets people where they live — low travel barrier  
- Builds sustained relationships with residents  
- Can integrate childcare, food distribution, and follow-up visits  

**Risks**
- Must obtain resident or management consent  
- Complex dynamics with security or HOAs  
- Privacy — residents may not want visibility  

**Tips**
- Partner with tenant leaders or resident councils  
- Use flyers or Signal check-ins before arrival  
- Keep gear minimal — folding chairs, handouts, small aid tables  

---

### ⛪ 4. Faith or Community Centers
**Best for:** Regular, reliable operations in shared spaces

**Strengths**
- Built-in trust and indoor facilities  
- Easier access to volunteers and electricity  
- Can host education or debrief sessions  

**Risks**
- May come with restrictions on language, topics, or political expression  
- Association with a faith group may alienate some community members  

**Tips**
- Clarify agreements about signage, language, and hours  
- Keep setup modular for easy teardown if needed  

---

### 🏢 5. Pop-Up Urban or Rural Lots
**Best for:** Emergency distribution and crisis response

**Strengths**
- Can be set up fast and independently  
- No permissions if abandoned or public easement  
- Low-profile operations possible  

**Risks**
- Security uncertainty; police or private guards may appear  
- Limited facilities, exposure to elements  
- Requires self-contained power and sanitation  

**Tips**
- Arrive as a team, not solo  
- Use vehicle-based lighting and power  
- Pack out all materials; avoid leaving traces

## 🧰 Full Setup-to-Teardown Checklist

### 🕐 Before Arrival
- [ ] Confirm weather and daylight hours  
- [ ] Verify permission or safe fallback location  
- [ ] Load gear: tables, banners, shade, water, med kit, sanitation  
- [ ] Print quick reference contact cards  
- [ ] Coordinate comms: Signal group or radio channel  
- [ ] Assign roles: Lead, Safety, Outreach, Logistics, Media (if any)  
- [ ] Bring extra: masks, gloves, snacks, hydration  

### 🚧 On Arrival / Setup
- [ ] Conduct 10-minute observation: traffic, police, public behavior  
- [ ] Identify at least 2 exit routes for vehicles and people  
- [ ] Set up banners and signs with clear, calm language  
- [ ] Keep medical and sanitation stations visible but not obstructive  
- [ ] Mark quiet zone for decompression or private conversation  
- [ ] Check wind direction before setting shade or tents  
- [ ] Log who’s on shift and who’s offsite backup  

### 🧡 During Operation
- [ ] Rotate tasks every 60–90 minutes  
- [ ] Watch for signs of stress or conflict  
- [ ] Log new contacts securely and anonymously if needed  
- [ ] Offer food, water, and rest first — then conversation or flyers  
- [ ] Debrief newcomers quickly but calmly  
- [ ] Maintain nonjudgmental tone; don’t force engagement  
- [ ] Regular check-ins: “Are we still safe? Is anyone uneasy?”  

### 🌙 Teardown / Demobilization
- [ ] Announce 15-minute warning for close-down  
- [ ] Confirm all gear packed and trash removed  
- [ ] Verify no sensitive materials left behind  
- [ ] Ensure all volunteers have transport home  
- [ ] Conduct 5-minute team debrief  
- [ ] Log any incidents or follow-ups  
- [ ] Message dispatch or regional coordinator with closure confirmation  

---

## ⚠️ Red-Line Conditions (Move or Abort)
- Law enforcement or private security targeting presence  
- Harassment from public or threats escalating  
- Extreme heat, cold, or lightning  
- Internal signals of burnout, confusion, or unease  
- Loss of comms or unverified visitor trying to lead team  

> Mobility and caution are not fear — they’re strategy.
