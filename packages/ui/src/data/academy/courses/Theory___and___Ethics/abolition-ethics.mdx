## Overview
Abolition is both **a moral stance** and a **daily practice**.  
It challenges the idea that harm must be met with punishment, insisting instead that **no one is disposable** and that safety comes from care and connection, not control.  
Modern abolition builds on the struggle against slavery and continues through fights against prisons, policing, and border regimes that cage and exclude.

---

## Learn & Do

### Key Terms
- **Carceral logic** — The belief that punishment is the proper response to harm.  
- **Transformative justice** — Addressing harm without incarceration or policing.  
- **Mutual aid** — Voluntary cooperation for shared benefit, defying state neglect.  
- **Disposability** — The idea that some people can be cast aside.  
- **Praxis** — The merging of theory and action into daily practice.

### What Is Abolition?
Abolition means:
- Ending systems of punishment and caging.  
- Rebuilding communities around care and accountability.  
- Refusing violence as justice.  

It calls us to imagine and practice responses to harm that **heal rather than punish**.

---

### Historical Roots
Modern abolition draws from:
- The **Black Radical Tradition**  
- Indigenous sovereignty movements  
- Feminist and queer liberation praxis  
- Restorative and transformative justice work

### Why Abolish ICE, Police, and Prisons?
These systems:
- Emerge from racial violence and colonial control  
- Fail to meet safety or care needs  
- Disproportionately harm marginalized communities  
- Reinforce hierarchies of disposability  

<Callout type="warning">
“Prisons do not disappear social problems, they disappear human beings.” — Angela Y. Davis  
</Callout>

### Ethical Logic of Abolition
Abolition asks:
- What are the root causes of harm?  
- How can we respond without reproducing violence?  
- What conditions make policing unnecessary?  

It shifts ethics from punishment to **collective repair and transformation**.

---

### Accountability Without Violence
| Harm Scenario | Carceral Response | Abolitionist Alternative |
|----------------|------------------|---------------------------|
| Theft | Call police, press charges | Restorative circle to repair relationships |
| Intimate partner violence | Arrest perpetrator | Safety planning and survivor-led intervention |
| Vandalism | Fines or jail | Repair labor + dialogue about grievances |

### Reform vs. Abolition
<Callout type="warning">
Reforms like body cameras and predictive policing often **expand budgets and legitimacy** instead of reducing harm.
</Callout>

Reforms strengthen punitive institutions.  
Abolition rejects that logic entirely and focuses on building new systems rooted in care.

---

### Abolition as Creation
Abolition is generative:
- **Instead of prisons:** Healing circles, housing, mental health care  
- **Instead of police:** Crisis teams, community patrols, de-escalators  
- **Instead of deportation:** Sanctuary networks, documentation aid  

> “Abolition is about presence, not absence. It's about building life-affirming institutions.” — Ruth Wilson Gilmore

### Practice-Based Ethics
Abolition lives in daily choices:
- Interrupt carceral language (“deserving,” “guilty”).  
- Replace exclusion with repair and return.  
- Use nonviolent communication.  
- Train in de-escalation.  
- Practice mutual care, not surveillance.  

**Pod Challenge:**  
Each week, identify one carceral habit and replace it with an abolitionist one.

---

### Disability, Class, and Global Justice
- **Disability Justice:** Institutions cage disabled people; abolition demands alternatives.  
- **Class:** Punishment targets the poor across all races.  
- **Global Solidarity:** Extends abolition to borders and detention centers.
