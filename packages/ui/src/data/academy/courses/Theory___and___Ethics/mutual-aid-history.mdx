## Overview
Mutual aid is the voluntary, cooperative sharing of resources for collective survival.  
It has always existed — from Indigenous reciprocity systems to modern resistance networks — adapting to crisis, colonization, and community defense.

Key principles:
- Solidarity over saviorism  
- Horizontal organization  
- Community-defined participation  
- Sustainability and care as resistance  

This course explores the historical evolution of mutual aid and connects those roots to present-day organizing.

---

## Learn & Do

### 1. Indigenous Foundations
Mutual aid predates the term itself.  
Indigenous nations across the world practiced reciprocal systems such as:
- **Potlatch ceremonies** (Pacific Northwest): Redistribution of food and wealth  
- **Ayni** (Quechua): Rotational labor exchanges  
- **Clan protection networks** (Diné, Hausa): Shared defense and support  

<Callout type="success">
Mutual aid is ancestral. It existed before colonization and survived attempts at erasure.
</Callout>

---

### 2. The Term “Mutual Aid”
In 1902, Russian anarchist **Peter Kropotkin** published *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution*, arguing that cooperation drives evolution as much as competition.

> “Sociability is as much a law of nature as mutual struggle.” — Peter Kropotkin

He gave academic language to practices long alive in Indigenous, peasant, and communal life.

---

### 3. Black Mutual Aid Societies
Black communities in the U.S. created networks of self-determination and survival:
- **Free African Society** (1787): Healthcare and burial support  
- **Underground Railroad:** Trust-based, decentralized escape network  
- **Black Panther Free Breakfast Program:** Fed tens of thousands of children daily  

These were not charity projects — they were liberation infrastructure.

---

### 4. Global Movements of Solidarity
Examples across the world:
- **Zapatista zones** (Mexico): Autonomous clinics, farms, and schools  
- **Palestinian neighborhood councils:** Aid under siege and blockade  
- **Korean peasant cooperatives:** Shared land defense and collective farming  

Each demonstrates how mutual aid sustains dignity where states fail.

---

### 5. COVID-19 Revival
In 2020, mutual aid resurged amid pandemic collapse:
- Neighborhood pods delivered groceries and medicine  
- Mask-making collectives produced millions of PPE units  
- Bail funds and rent relief networks expanded nationally  

📍 **Case Study: East Oakland Collective**  
Coordinated 5,000+ deliveries, prioritizing elders and undocumented families.  
Used encrypted comms and paper records to reduce surveillance risk.

> “We keep us safe.” wasn’t a slogan — it was infrastructure.

---

### 6. Charity vs. Mutual Aid

| Charity | Mutual Aid |
|----------|-------------|
| Top-down giver/recipient | Peer-to-peer solidarity |
| Maintains status quo | Challenges systemic failure |
| Often conditional | Unconditional and community-defined |
| Temporary relief | Structural change and shared survival |

---

### 7. Practicing Modern Mutual Aid
Today’s pods use:
- Encrypted comms (Signal, SimpleX, Briar)  
- Decentralized spreadsheets or apps for logistics  
- Court support, translation, and supply distribution  
- Safety and de-escalation crews  

<Callout type="warning">
Digital tools are vulnerable to surveillance.  
Security culture is care, not paranoia.
</Callout>

<Callout type="hint">
Follow [Documentation Safety](/courses/documentation-safety) and [After-Action Data Hygiene](/courses/after-action-data-hygiene).  
Store no unnecessary names, locations, or records without consent.
</Callout>

---

### 8. Lessons from the Lineage
- 🔁 **Sustainability:** Avoid burnout by sharing roles and rest cycles  
- 🛡️ **Security:** Assume repression is possible, plan accordingly  
- 🪶 **Storytelling:** Preserve oral and written histories to sustain culture and morale
