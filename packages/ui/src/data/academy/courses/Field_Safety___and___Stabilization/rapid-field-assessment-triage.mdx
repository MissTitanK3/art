# 🚑 Rapid Field Assessment & Triage for Civilian Responders
<TrackBadge variant="field-safety" />

<Callout type="info">
When something goes wrong in the field, the first five minutes matter most. This course teaches how to size up a situation, keep yourself safe, and communicate clearly while prioritizing care.
</Callout>

---

## Overview
Civilian responders are often first on scene during crises—before medics, dispatchers, or coordinated aid arrive. In those moments, **calm observation and structured triage** can save lives and prevent secondary harm.

This course focuses on:
- Rapid scene assessment and personal safety.  
- Identifying urgent vs. stable needs.  
- Using simple, non-medical triage models.  
- Communicating structured updates to dispatch or medics.  

It is not medical training or certification. It’s a framework to help mutual aid volunteers **stabilize chaos long enough for organized response to begin**.

---

## Learn & Do

✅ **1. Scene safety comes first**
- Do not rush in. Stop, breathe, and scan.  
- Ask: *Is it safe for me to enter?* Look for traffic, violence, electrical hazards, or gas leaks.  
- If unsafe, retreat and report immediately to dispatch.  
- Use environmental cover and stay visible when needed.

🔍 *Task:* Practice scanning an area for 10 seconds and listing potential hazards before approaching.

---

✅ **2. Perform a 10-second scene size-up**
Ask yourself:
1. **What happened?** (Fire, accident, heat injury, etc.)  
2. **How many people are involved?**  
3. **What resources are needed?** (Medical, security, supplies)  
4. **Is there an ongoing threat?**  
5. **Who else is already responding?**

🧩 *Example:* Arriving at a small march where someone has fainted. You check for crowd density, sun exposure, and traffic before approaching, then notify dispatch: “One person fainted, heat likely, scene safe, bystanders assisting.”

---

✅ **3. Use simple triage cues**
When more than one person is affected, prioritize using **START-like logic (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment)** adapted for civilian use:

| Priority | Condition | Example |
|-----------|------------|----------|
| 🔴 **Immediate (Red)** | Life-threatening but treatable if acted on now. | Severe bleeding, unconscious breathing, heat stroke. |
| 🟡 **Delayed (Yellow)** | Needs help soon, not critical. | Minor fracture, moderate dehydration. |
| 🟢 **Minor (Green)** | Walking, talking, mobile. | Scrapes, mild anxiety, exhaustion. |
| ⚫ **Deceased / No signs of life** | No breathing or response after initial check. | Do not move unless needed for safety. |

⚙️ *Task:* Print or memorize this color code. Use wristbands, tape, or paper tags in real scenarios.

---

✅ **4. Communicate structured updates**
Use the **METHANE** format (modified for mutual aid):
- **M**ajor incident?  
- **E**xact location.  
- **T**ype of incident.  
- **H**azards present.  
- **A**ccess routes or blockages.  
- **N**umber of affected people.  
- **E**mergency needs or resources requested.

Example message:  
> “Mutual Aid Tacoma, Pine & 10th, vehicle collision, fluid on road, 2 injured (1 red, 1 yellow), no visible fire, need first aid kit and traffic control.”

---

✅ **5. Manage stress and stay clear-headed**
- Slow your breathing before transmitting info.  
- Speak only what you’ve confirmed.  
- Use simple language—avoid “code” unless your region standardizes it.  
- After events, debrief and decompress to prevent emotional overload.

<Callout type="warning">
⚠️ Never perform medical interventions beyond your training level.  
Focus on safety, information relay, and emotional steadiness.
</Callout>

---

✅ **6. Transition control**
When medics or trained responders arrive:
- Identify yourself and summarize the scene clearly.  
- Hand off any written notes or triage tags.  
- Step back unless requested to assist.  
- Document and relay updates for After-Action Review (AAR).
