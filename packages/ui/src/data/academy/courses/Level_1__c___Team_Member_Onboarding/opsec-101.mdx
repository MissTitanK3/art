## Overview

Every activist, dispatcher, or volunteer handles information that can be weaponized.  
Phones, chats, meeting notes, and simple habits leave trails.  
**Good OPSEC doesn’t hide everything — it minimizes harm when leaks happen.**

This course gives you frameworks and tools to protect people, not paranoia.

## The OPSEC 5-Step Framework

| Step | Purpose |
|------|----------|
| **1️⃣ Identify Critical Info** | What would cause harm if exposed? |
| **2️⃣ Analyze Threats** | Who wants it, and what can they do? |
| **3️⃣ Find Vulnerabilities** | Where and how leaks happen |
| **4️⃣ Match Countermeasures** | Balance cost, risk, and realism |
| **5️⃣ Implement & Repeat** | Apply, test, and update regularly |

<Callout type="info">
OPSEC isn’t a one-time task — it’s a living system that evolves with your team.
</Callout>

---

## Step 1: Identify What Needs Protection

Ask:
- What are we doing that could be targeted?  
- Who would be harmed if this were public?  
- What would exposure ruin — the plan, the people, or trust itself?

Examples:
- Meeting times, routes, or supply drops  
- Member identities and contact info  
- Communications tools and channel access  
- Pod and dispatch structures  

---

## Step 2: Analyze the Threats

Build your **threat model**:
- Who’s watching? (Law enforcement, far-right groups, data brokers)  
- What tools do they use? (Facial recognition, social scraping, GPS)  
- What do they already know, and how might they learn more?  

Useful reads:
- [EFF: NSA Surveillance Overview](https://www.eff.org/nsa-spying)  
- [Standing Rock Infiltration Leaks](https://theintercept.com/)  
- [Global Surveillance Disclosures 2013–Present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_surveillance_disclosures_(2013%E2%80%93present))

<Callout type="warning">
Don’t assume you’re too small to be monitored.  
History shows surveillance targets the most effective and vulnerable first.
</Callout>

---

## Step 3: Identify Vulnerabilities

| Info Type | Leak Vector | Example |
|------------|--------------|----------|
| Location | Device tracking | GPS, Wi-Fi, cell tower triangulation |
| Identity | Social media | Photos, tags, usernames |
| Comms | Apps | SMS and many DMs are plaintext |
| Movement | Purchases, transport | Cards, rideshare, license plates |

Next: match each leak to a specific mitigation.

---

## Step 4: Choose Countermeasures

| Category | Strategy | Example |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| 🚫 **Action Control** | Remove the risk entirely | Don’t bring phones to meetings |
| 🥷 **Countermeasure** | Mask or disrupt data | Faraday bags, burner devices |
| 🎭 **Counter-Analysis** | Confuse tracking | Decoy phones, alternate routes |

> Example: Give your regular phone to a friend moving elsewhere while you travel offline.  
> Tracking software will follow the wrong trail.

<Callout type="info">
Always test countermeasures before relying on them.  
A broken or misused tool is worse than none.
</Callout>

---

## Step 5: Implement and Adapt

- Keep personal and operational identities separate  
- Rotate devices and accounts regularly  
- Use Signal groups with aliases, not real names  
- Mask repeat travel or posting patterns  
- Conduct **post-action OPSEC reviews** after every major event  

<Callout type="hint">
Build OPSEC steps into deployment checklists and pod briefings.  
Routine makes protection automatic.
</Callout>

---

## Building Security Culture

Strong OPSEC is collective:
- Don’t brag about actions or share sensitive info  
- Teach new members early, without shaming  
- Normalize encrypted tools and cautious posting  
- Challenge “it’s fine, no one’s watching” assumptions  

> Culture beats compliance. Everyone protects everyone else.

<Callout type="hint">
Documentation hygiene:  
Keep retention short. Use encrypted note apps.  
Delete logs after debriefs.  
ART does **not** archive OPSEC notes or private messages.
</Callout>

---

## Practice Scenario: Phone Security

🔍 Situation: You’re coordinating a protest. Communication must stay secure.

Recommended plan:
1. Use **burner Signal groups** with aliases.  
2. Share logistics only at the last minute.  
3. Keep personal phones powered off or elsewhere.  
4. Relay via radio or in-person messengers when possible.  
5. Review who has which data after the action.

## Tools and Layers

| Tool | Use Case |
|------|-----------|
| **Signal** | Encrypted text and calls |
| **Faraday Bag** | Block all device signals |
| **GrapheneOS / CalyxOS** | Hardened Android variants |
| **Offline Radios / LoRa Mesh** | Infrastructure-free comms |
| **Tails OS** | Private, USB-only desktop OS |
| **Tor or VPN** | Mask IP and metadata |

> No tool replaces discipline.  
> OPSEC fails from behavior, not from software.

## OPSEC Checklist

### Before Action
- [ ] Identify sensitive information  
- [ ] Define adversaries and threats  
- [ ] Scan for leaks or exposed habits  
- [ ] Prepare burners and aliases  

### During
- [ ] Create only essential data  
- [ ] Keep to “need-to-know” channels  
- [ ] Track who knows what and why  
- [ ] Watch for suspicious digital behavior  

### After
- [ ] Wipe or rotate devices  
- [ ] Debrief and update pod protocols  
- [ ] Archive safely or delete intentionally
