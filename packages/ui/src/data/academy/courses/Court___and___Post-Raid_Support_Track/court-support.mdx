# Court Support Essentials

Showing up for someone in court is an act of protection, solidarity, and love. This guide will prepare you to confidently and responsibly provide court support in both immigration and criminal court contexts.
---
<Callout type="info">
⚖️ 75% of detained immigrants face court without legal representation. Your presence reminds the system someone is watching.
</Callout>
---
<Callout type="important">
📌 This training prepares you for accompaniment and observation—not for giving legal advice or testifying.
</Callout>

<Callout type="hint">
If you’ll be coordinating with organizers or dispatch, align handoffs and safety signals with <a href="/courses/responding-to-dispatch-calls">Responding to Dispatch Calls</a> and review consent norms in <a href="/courses/community-consent">Community Consent</a>.
</Callout>

## What Is Court Support?

Court support means physically or emotionally showing up for someone facing legal proceedings. It can include:

- Accompanying someone to court
- Sitting silently in the courtroom
- Helping with transportation or childcare
- Translating or explaining court processes
- Providing moral support before/after hearings

Even when it seems small, court support reminds our people: **you are not alone.**

---

## Before the Hearing

### Gather Information

- Know the time and courtroom number
- Understand the type of hearing (e.g., arraignment, bond, trial)
- Confirm names and pronouns of the person you're supporting
- Know whether it’s immigration or criminal court

<Callout type="info">
If language support may be needed, plan ahead. See the <a href="/courses/translator-micro-badge">Translator Micro‑badge</a> and <a href="/courses/asl-interpreter-micro-badge">ASL Interpreter Micro‑badge</a>.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
⚠️ Some courts prohibit phones, metal objects, or bags. Know what you can bring in before arriving.
</Callout>

### Prep Logistics

- Coordinate arrival time and meet-up spot
- Arrange interpreters or childcare if needed
- Bring ID if required to enter the building
- **Dress respectfully** – Avoid slogans, overly casual wear, or all-black protest gear. Business-casual is best.

---

## During the Hearing

### Your Role Is Silent Power

- Sit respectfully and silently in the courtroom
- Make eye contact with the person you’re supporting
- Use non-verbal signals of presence (e.g., hand on heart)
- Avoid visible emotional reactions—even to injustice

<Callout type="warning">
⚠️ Some courts ban note-taking. If you take notes, do so discreetly and respectfully.
</Callout>

### Do NOT:

- Speak unless addressed by court staff or the judge
- Record audio/video (almost always prohibited)
- Bring protest signs or confront officers
- Act as a legal advisor or interpreter unless qualified

<Callout type="hint">
If documentation is allowable and truly necessary, follow <a href="/courses/documentation-safety">Documentation Safety</a> and never post without explicit consent and legal review.
</Callout>

---

## After the Hearing

- Check in emotionally with the person you supported
- Offer to walk them out, get food, or sit quietly
- Help them debrief—“What do you remember?” instead of “What now?”
- Review any next steps: paperwork, deadlines, future hearings

<Callout type="info">
Plan the follow‑through with the legal team and support network. See <a href="/courses/legal-followthrough-jail-support">Legal Follow‑through & Jail Support</a> and coordinate emotional support with <a href="/courses/care-support">Care & Emotional Support</a>.
</Callout>

<Callout type="success">
💬 Don’t try to explain the law unless you're trained. “I'm with you—let’s figure this out together” is powerful.
</Callout>

## Common Scenarios

| Scenario                             | Suggested Support                                  |
|-------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Courtroom too full                  | Show presence in the hallway or outside            |
| Person is detained                  | Speak to their lawyer before/after for updates     |
| Emotional breakdown                 | Offer grounding after court, not during            |
| No interpreter present              | Notify clerk or defense team, don’t intervene      |

## Immigration vs. Criminal Court

| Feature       | Immigration Court                | Criminal Court                |
|---------------|----------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Judges        | Appointed                        | Elected or appointed          |
| Legal Rights  | No public defender guaranteed    | Right to an attorney if poor  |
| Main Goal     | Deportation                      | Punishment or release         |
| Language Use  | Often lacks interpretation       | Interpreters more common      |
| Public Access | Often closed                     | Typically public              |

## Safety & Consent

- **Always ask** if the person wants you to attend
- **Never post** about court without clear consent
- **Respect privacy**, even in solidarity

<Callout type="warning">
🔒 Sharing names, faces, or court info can lead to retaliation or legal harm—even if your intent is supportive.
</Callout>

<div className="p-4 rounded">
  <p><strong>Before Posting About a Hearing:</strong></p>
  <p>✅ Ask: “Is it okay if I share this? How much detail?”</p>
  <p>❌ Avoid: Names, photos, case numbers, locations</p>
  <p>⚠️ Even positive posts can be used by prosecutors</p>
</div>


---

## If Something Goes Wrong

- If ICE arrests someone after court, **do not film** — alert trusted legal contacts or community hotlines immediately.
- If you’re asked to leave the courtroom or courthouse, **do so calmly** and regroup with others outside.
- If someone is targeted, harassed, or mistreated in court, **document safely** (if permitted) and **report to your support coordinator or legal team—not social media**.

<Callout type="warning">
🚨 Even small mistakes can escalate tension or risk. Your calm response helps protect everyone involved.
</Callout>

<Callout type="hint">
Know your rights when questioned or searched in court facilities. Review <a href="/courses/kyr-basics">Know Your Rights (KYR) Basics</a>.
</Callout>

## Steps to Become a Court Supporter

1. Attend or observe a court session anonymously.
2. Connect with local groups who organize regular court support.
3. Learn the basic case types (e.g., arraignment, bond, trial) and court timelines.
4. Shadow experienced volunteers before going alone.
5. Understand trauma responses and know your personal limits.

<Callout type="info">
After any court support action, log a brief debrief and handle any notes securely. See <a href="/courses/after-action-data-hygiene">After‑Action Data Hygiene</a>.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Court support is sacred, steady work rooted in presence and consent.
- Your job is to show up—**quietly, attentively, and respectfully**.
- Prepare ahead of time. Sit with care. Debrief afterward.
- **Ask for consent. Ask again. Stay humble.**
