# 🚶‍♀️ Wellness Float: Mobile Observation & Preventative Care
<TrackBadge variant="specialized-role" />

<Callout type="info">
Wellness Floats keep the movement healthy and alert. They move quietly between teams, sensing strain before it escalates, offering water, grounding, or direction toward rest.
</Callout>

---

## Overview
During high-activity events, stress builds invisibly. **Wellness Floats** act as roving caregivers—bridging communication between ground teams, medics, and dispatch to ensure no one burns out, overheats, or isolates unnoticed.  

They combine **calm presence, social awareness, and soft authority** to maintain collective stability. Their presence alone signals care and readiness, reinforcing psychological safety across the field.  

You’ll learn to:
- Recognize early burnout or distress.  
- Offer small interventions that prevent collapse.  
- Communicate fluidly with dispatch and other roles.  
- Keep yourself emotionally and physically regulated.

---

## Learn & Do

✅ **1. Prepare your route and tools**
- Coordinate with Dispatch before each patrol:  
  - Zones to visit  
  - Duration and frequency  
  - Key individuals or teams needing check-ins  
- Pack essentials:
  - Water, electrolytes, snacks  
  - Sunscreen, cooling cloth, electrolyte powder  
  - Basic first-aid kit, tissues, and grounding tools  
  - Radio or encrypted messaging device  
- Move light and unobtrusive—blend into flow.

🔍 *Task:* Design a personal “Float Kit” that fits in a small sling bag with items to cover both physical and emotional care needs.

---

✅ **2. Read the room (or crowd)**
- Observe posture, tone, and body language:
  - Are people quiet or snappy?  
  - Has anyone withdrawn or stopped responding?  
  - Are volunteers skipping breaks or hydration?  
- Scan for visible stress:
  - Red faces, tremors, pacing, flat affect.  
- Ask simple, open questions:
  - “How are you holding up?”  
  - “Have you had water lately?”  
- Watch your own pace—your calm is contagious.

🧩 *Example:* You notice a small group arguing at a supply tent. You approach slowly, hand out water, and defuse tension by redirecting focus to next tasks.

✅ **3. Offer low-intervention support**
- Use gentle tone and physical space awareness.  
- Offer resources, not instructions:
  - “Would you like to take a quick break in shade?”  
  - “There’s water at the Care Station if you need a reset.”  
- Ground through the senses:
  - Offer a cool compress, water, or quiet breathing moment.  
- Avoid overstepping—never force care or insist on talking.

<Callout type="warning">
⚠️ Never diagnose or assume motive.  
Your goal is to stabilize, not interpret behavior.
</Callout>

---

✅ **4. Coordinate with Dispatch and specialized roles**
- Report patterns, not personal details:
  - “Three volunteers at Site B showing fatigue.”  
  - “Recommend water resupply to Zone 4.”  
- Escalate if:
  - Someone collapses, vomits, or stops responding.  
  - Mental health crisis signs appear.  
  - Crowd energy spikes toward aggression or despair.  
- Keep transmissions brief and coded for privacy.  

⚙️ *Tip:* Pair with **Care Station** or **Medic leads** for shared situational awareness.

---

✅ **5. Manage your own pacing**
- Floaters often overextend. Plan rest checkpoints every 60–90 minutes.  
- Hydrate every 20 minutes; set alarms if needed.  
- If emotional fatigue sets in:
  - Hand off radio temporarily.  
  - Take 10 minutes alone to breathe and reset.  
- Never float solo for extended hours without check-ins.

🧩 *Example:* Mid-afternoon, you realize you’re dizzy from heat. You radio, “Float 3 taking 10 for hydration—back on loop in ten.” That model sets healthy norms for others.

---

✅ **6. Support debrief and recovery**
- Share short factual notes post-shift:
  - Zones covered  
  - Issues observed  
  - Supply needs or morale notes  
- Avoid gossip or speculation.  
- Debrief with mental health or Care teams if you witnessed heavy distress.  
- Rest, stretch, hydrate, and disconnect before sleeping.

🔍 *Task:* Create a short reflection log template to record emotional tone, lessons learned, and recommended adjustments for next event.
