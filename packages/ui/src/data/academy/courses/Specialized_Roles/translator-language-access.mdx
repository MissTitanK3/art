# 🌐 Translator: Language Access & Communication Equity
<TrackBadge variant="specialized-role" />

<Callout type="info">
Translators make solidarity possible across language barriers. They turn confusion into connection, ensuring that every person—regardless of language—can understand, decide, and act in real time.
</Callout>

---

## Overview
Language is infrastructure. During actions and organizing, **Translators** enable communication between multilingual teams, participants, and communities. Their precision and neutrality ensure that critical information—legal rights, medical needs, or tactical updates—is understood by all.

Translators are responsible for:
- Real-time translation in high-stakes settings.  
- Accurate written translation of statements, signage, or dispatch messages.  
- Maintaining confidentiality and context accuracy.  
- Supporting inclusive communication planning before events.

---

## Learn & Do

✅ **1. Understand your ethical role**
- Translate meaning, not opinion.  
- Stay neutral—avoid inserting commentary or omitting sensitive terms.  
- Protect confidentiality at all times.  
- If unsure of a term, clarify before proceeding rather than guessing.  
- Translate *tone* when relevant (“urgent,” “calm,” “official”).  

🔍 *Task:* Draft a short translator’s oath or code of ethics for your region’s multilingual team.

---

✅ **2. Prepare before deployment**
- Confirm which languages and dialects will be needed.  
- Review expected vocabulary: legal, medical, logistical, or dispatch-specific.  
- Carry:
  - Notepad or translation app (offline-capable).  
  - Quick glossary of local terms (acronyms, landmarks, agency names).  
  - Headphones or mic if assisting in noisy environments.  
- Coordinate with Dispatch, Legal Support, or Safety Marshals to plan where translation is most needed.

🧩 *Example:* Before a rally, you meet with the team to learn chants, review safety signals, and pre-translate common emergency instructions into multiple languages.

✅ **3. On-the-spot spoken translation**
- Stand close enough to hear both parties but maintain personal space.  
- Speak clearly, one sentence behind the speaker if possible.  
- Match tone and volume—soft voice for calm moments, steady for instructions.  
- If conversation escalates emotionally, keep your own tone even and measured.  
- When translating for officials or press, include all words—never censor criticism or slang unless asked to moderate by both parties.  

⚙️ *Tip:* Clarify ambiguity immediately—misinterpretation can create risk.

---

✅ **4. Written and digital translation**
- Translate dispatch messages, signage, and alerts consistently.  
- Verify context: a single word like “secure” may mean “safe” or “locked.”  
- Use inclusive language—avoid gendered or hierarchical phrasing where unnecessary.  
- For public documents, request peer review from another fluent translator.  
- Keep sensitive translations (legal, medical, personal data) off public channels.  

🧩 *Example:* A field alert says, “Evacuate east gate, regroup at Lot B.” You ensure the translated message conveys both direction and urgency exactly.

---

✅ **5. Support accessibility in group settings**
- Repeat or summarize key points for multilingual participants.  
- Encourage slower speech and pauses during meetings.  
- Coordinate turns between multiple translators if languages overlap.  
- Prepare summary notes or text recaps for deaf or hard-of-hearing participants.  
- Work with ASL interpreters and captioners to form a unified communication network.

<Callout type="warning">
⚠️ Avoid becoming a gatekeeper of information.  
Your task is to empower—not filter—communication.
</Callout>

---

✅ **6. Handle high-stress translation**
In urgent settings (medical incidents, police contact, evacuations):
- Focus on clarity, not perfection.  
- Use short, direct phrasing.  
- Repeat back key instructions to confirm understanding.  
- Translate emotional reassurance when possible (“You’re safe,” “Help is coming”).  
- Debrief with your team afterward to check accuracy and share improvements.

🧩 *Example:* You interpret between a medic and a non-English speaker during tear gas exposure—conveying calm breathing guidance and injury symptoms with precision.

---

✅ **7. Practice confidentiality and self-care**
- Do not discuss or share translated content beyond its purpose.  
- Protect notes, devices, and recordings with encryption.  
- Recognize emotional fatigue—hearing trauma in multiple languages can be draining.  
- Rotate duties with other translators to maintain focus and accuracy.
