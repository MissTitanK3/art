# ♿ Disability Aid: Accessibility & Inclusive Field Support
<TrackBadge variant="specialized-role" />

<Callout type="info">
Disability Aid volunteers make collective action possible for everyone. They remove barriers, adapt logistics, and ensure that all community members can participate with safety and dignity.
</Callout>

---

## Overview
**Disability Aid** is not charity—it’s solidarity. These volunteers help create accessible, inclusive spaces where disabled people can fully participate in actions, mutual aid operations, and recovery work.  

This role combines **awareness, adaptation, and discretion**. It involves everything from helping someone navigate terrain to advocating for accessibility in planning meetings.  

You’ll learn how to:
- Identify and remove environmental and communication barriers.  
- Support disabled participants respectfully and safely.  
- Prepare accessibility plans for actions and CDC sites.  
- Uphold disability justice principles across operations.

---

## Learn & Do

✅ **1. Understand disability justice principles**
- Center **interdependence**, not independence.  
- Assume **access needs are universal**, not exceptional.  
- Recognize that ableism compounds with other oppressions—race, class, gender.  
- Nothing about disabled people **without** disabled people.  
- Accessibility is safety.

🔍 *Task:* Reflect on your own access needs and how others could support you during an action. This builds empathy and foresight.

---

✅ **2. Identify and plan for access needs**
Before an event or deployment:
- Assess venue terrain—stairs, ramps, lighting, rest zones, accessible toilets.  
- Designate quiet or low-sensory spaces.  
- Coordinate transportation and wheelchair access.  
- Ensure medics and safety leads know how to assist mobility-impaired or neurodivergent participants.  
- Create alternate communication channels (text-based, visual, ASL).

---

✅ **3. Provide physical or sensory support safely**
- Always ask **before** offering help. (“Would you like support on your left side or right?”)  
- Let the person lead; never push or pull mobility aids without consent.  
- Offer your arm or walk slightly ahead to guide visually impaired individuals.  
- Announce environmental changes (“Curb coming up in three feet”).  
- Keep communication clear, concise, and calm.  
- Carry spare earplugs, noise-cancelling headphones, and visual cards for sensory regulation.

⚙️ *Tip:* Respect autonomy—assist only as much as needed and step back when no longer required.

---

✅ **4. Support neurodiverse and sensory needs**
- Minimize sudden noises or physical contact.  
- Provide clear, step-by-step explanations of what’s happening.  
- Offer quiet time or stimming breaks.  
- If someone is overstimulated:
  - Move them to a quieter space.  
  - Encourage deep breathing or grounding focus (object, texture, color).  
  - Avoid crowds, bright lights, and direct touch.  

🔍 *Task:* Prepare a short “sensory toolkit” with sunglasses, headphones, and small fidgets to share at events.

---

✅ **5. Act as an accessibility advocate**
- Speak up during planning when barriers appear (stairs-only venues, loud alarms, unclear instructions).  
- Remind teams that accessibility benefits everyone—especially in emergencies.  
- Encourage universal design in signage, layout, and communication tools.  
- Ensure that deaf or hard-of-hearing participants can access safety announcements and debriefs.  

🧩 *Example:* You notice a radio channel without captioning for key updates. You coordinate with the Comms team to provide a text-based relay during operations.

---

✅ **6. Practice privacy and discretion**
- Never share a person’s medical or disability information without explicit consent.  
- Avoid labeling participants by condition or need.  
- Speak respectfully, directly, and privately when discussing support.  
- Remember: visibility ≠ consent to disclose.

<Callout type="warning">
⚠️ Breaching confidentiality can cause emotional harm or public risk.  
Protect information with the same care as sensitive dispatch data.
</Callout>

---

✅ **7. Post-event care and feedback**
- Check in with disabled participants afterward. Ask:
  - What worked?  
  - What barriers remained?  
  - What can improve next time?  
- Document accessibility lessons learned in After-Action Reviews.  
- Restock accessibility supplies and repair adaptive equipment if needed.
